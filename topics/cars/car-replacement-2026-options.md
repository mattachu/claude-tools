# Dad's Car Replacement 2026 — Background & Options Considered

This is the reasoning and shortlist that led to [the decision recorded in car-replacement-2026.md](car-replacement-2026.md). Kept for reference if the current purchase falls through or the reasoning needs revisiting — not needed for day-to-day advice on completing the current purchase.

## Original situation
Dad's BMW 320i (2022) lease was ending. Purchase price to keep it: ~£23,000. Dad was originally inclined to buy it and keep driving it. Matt preferred a hybrid or EV instead, for environmental reasons. The car is shared: **Dad, Farrah, and Matt all drive it**; they live together.

## Household requirements
- Must seat **three adults comfortably in the back** — the two children (16 and 13) are both taller than Farrah, so treat all rear-seat occupants as adults, not kids. This ruled out several small hatchback EVs/hybrids with tight rear seats (e.g. Renault 5 E-Tech, Toyota Corolla Hybrid).
- Dad is used to BMW-level ride comfort and refinement; budget-brand options (MG, Dacia) are a likely step down and were deprioritised for that reason.
- Budget benchmark: ~£23,000 (what the 320i purchase would have cost) — the car eventually chosen matched this almost exactly.

## Usage pattern (established facts)
- **Actual annual mileage is ~10,200 miles/year**, confirmed via odometer (36,999.5 miles since Feb 2023) — a BMW-app "average mileage" figure of ~4,000/year was found to be wrong/filtered and should be disregarded.
- Mostly short local trips (all under ~10 miles from home): church (~3 miles each way, a few times/week), supermarket, gym, music clubs. No school runs (kids walk).
- A ~120-mile round trip most weeks or fortnights: to Banbury (Farrah's parents) or occasionally Basildon (Ben's family).
- Dad separately drives to Enfield roughly every 6 weeks (distance not specified).
- A couple of 200+ mile trips per year (holidays) — charging stops can be planned in advance for these.

## Environmental conclusion reached
- Because the current 320i would be resold (not scrapped) if Dad doesn't buy it, the "wasted embodied carbon" concern from scrappage studies doesn't apply — buying new elsewhere doesn't waste the BMW's production footprint.
- At ~10,200 miles/year, this sits well above the ~4,383 mile/year threshold (from a 2026 *Science* study) below which a new EV's manufacturing emissions aren't repaid — so replacing with a lower-emission car is environmentally favourable, not borderline.
- **This pushed the search toward a used PHEV, EV, or self-charging hybrid** rather than settling the debate at "just keep the old car."

## Why a used BMW 330e (PHEV) specifically
- Matches Dad's comfort expectations (same G20 3-Series platform/trim as his current car) and rear-seat space is a known quantity (same body as the existing 320i).
- Modelled electric-mileage share: ~65% of annual mileage could run electric in best case (assumes ~27 real-world miles/charge), rising to ~72% if topped up via a 3-pin socket (2.3kW) during visits to Banbury/Basildon.
- Key requirement for this to work: consistent nightly charging across three different drivers — Matt believes this is achievable.
- Known risk points on any used 330e: HV battery warranty remaining, AC-compressor/battery-cooling health (documented BMW TSB — compressor failure can contaminate battery cooling and accelerate degradation), and whether the 12V aux battery (a common, cheap, unrelated fault) is being confused with a genuine HV fault.

## Alternatives considered and set aside
**Used EVs (~£20k–£26k)** — best rear-seat/comfort matches identified:
- **Hyundai Ioniq 5** (~£20–22k+) — top pick so far for adult rear-seat space and comfort closest to BMW expectations
- Kia EV6 (~£22–23k) — similar platform, slightly tighter rear headroom
- Tesla Model 3 (£16–23k) — spacious, but interior finish/feel is more divisive
- Kia Niro EV (<£20k) — practical budget option, less premium feel
- Advantage over the PHEV: can cover both short *and* long trips electrically (with planned charging), not just short ones.

**Used self-charging (non-plug-in) hybrids** — no charging-discipline dependency, but always burn some petrol (they cannot run electric-only for meaningful distances):
- **Toyota RAV4 Hybrid** and **Honda CR-V Hybrid** — best all-rounders for space/refinement among mainstream options
- Kia Niro Hybrid — spacious but less premium
- **Lexus NX 300h** — closest comfort match to BMW refinement, but real-world economy is notably lower (~32–36 US mpg) than RAV4/Niro; 8–10 year old cars available at £10–14k (same core generation as newer ones, mostly minor known issues, no widespread battery failure pattern); 2021 cars run ~£26k, slightly over budget
- Note: BMW does **not** make self-charging hybrids — only PHEVs and 48V mild-hybrid petrol models — so a BMW-badged self-charging option doesn't exist.

## Background facts resolved (don't need to re-litigate)
- PHEVs cost *more* new than equivalent self-charging hybrids, but depreciate faster used (partly due to skepticism about unplugged company-car PHEVs) — explains why used 330e prices look competitive.
- EV battery degradation generally modest (~2.3%/year average, ~80% capacity retained after 8 years per large real-world study); BMW 330e-specific degradation reported at 5–10% over 5–7 years.
- BMW's battery warranty is a 70%-capacity guarantee, not a lifespan estimate; it transfers to subsequent owners.
- BMW's PHEV HV battery warranty is **6 years/62,000 miles** for Generation 3/4 batteries and **8 years/100,000 miles** for Generation 5 (19.5kWh, introduced May 2024) — confirm which generation applies to any specific car via a BMW dealer VIN lookup, since sellers can be mistaken about this.
